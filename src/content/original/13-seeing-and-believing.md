# Seeing and Believing

## Seeing Is Not Passive

We tend to think of vision as a passive process — the eyes take in light, and the brain produces an image. Minsky challenges this view fundamentally. Seeing is an active, constructive process in which the mind does not simply record what is there but actively builds a model of the visual world, filling in gaps, resolving ambiguities, and imposing structure based on expectations and prior knowledge.

Consider what happens when you look at a room. Your eyes can focus on only a small area at a time, yet you experience a complete, detailed scene. This is because your visual agents are constantly supplementing the limited input from the eyes with information from memory and expectation. You "see" the back of the bookshelf even though only the front is visible. You "see" complete objects even when they are partially hidden behind other objects. What you experience as "seeing" is largely a construction.

> "We think we see the world as it is. But what we actually see is a story our minds compose, using only a few clues from our eyes and a great many assumptions from our experience."

## Expectations and Illusions

The constructive nature of vision explains why optical illusions are possible. Illusions are not errors — they are cases where the mind's normal constructive processes produce results that diverge from physical reality. The Muller-Lyer illusion (two lines of equal length that appear unequal because of the arrowheads at their ends) works because the visual agents that estimate length are influenced by contextual cues that normally correlate with depth and distance.

More broadly, expectations shape what we see. If you expect to see a friend's face in a crowd, you may "see" it even when the person is not there — because your face-recognition agents, primed by expectation, can be triggered by a rough approximation. This phenomenon connects to the discussion of frames in Chapter 24: visual perception uses frame-like structures that provide default expectations, which are then confirmed or modified by incoming sensory data.

## Seeing What We Believe

The relationship between seeing and believing runs in both directions. Not only does what we see shape what we believe, but what we believe shapes what we see. This is not merely a metaphor. The agents that process visual information are influenced by higher-level agents that represent beliefs, goals, and expectations. A radiologist examining an X-ray sees patterns that an untrained person cannot see — not because the radiologist's eyes are better, but because the radiologist's visual agents have been trained to detect and interpret specific patterns.

This means that perception is never neutral. It is always shaped by the perceiver's history, knowledge, and current mental state. Two people looking at the same scene may literally see different things, because their visual agents are constructing different models based on different prior knowledge.

## The Collaboration of Perception and Cognition

Minsky rejects the traditional separation between perception and cognition — between seeing and thinking. In the society of mind, the same agents that participate in perception also participate in thought. The agents that recognize shapes in the visual field are related to the agents that imagine shapes when you think about geometry. The agents that parse a visual scene into objects and relationships are related to the agents that parse a sentence into words and meanings (Chapter 26).

This deep connection between seeing and thinking explains why visual metaphors are so powerful in communication and reasoning. We "see" the point of an argument, "look" at a problem from different angles, and "illuminate" difficult concepts. These are not just figures of speech — they reflect the genuine involvement of visual processing agents in abstract thought.
