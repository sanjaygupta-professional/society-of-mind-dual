# Censors and Jokes

## The Censor Agents

Not all agents in the society of mind are constructive. Some are specifically designed to suppress — to prevent certain thoughts, actions, or lines of reasoning from being pursued. Minsky calls these agents **censors**. Censors are among the most important agents in the mind, because without them, thinking would be overwhelmed by a flood of irrelevant, harmful, or counterproductive ideas.

A censor does not generate ideas — it recognizes and blocks bad ones. When you are about to say something inappropriate, a social censor suppresses the utterance before it reaches your mouth. When you are reasoning about a problem and start down a path that has failed before, a reasoning censor blocks that path and redirects your thinking. Censors are the negative knowledge of the society of mind — they encode what not to do, which is often more valuable than knowing what to do.

> "Much of what we call 'good judgment' comes not from knowing the right answer, but from having learned to suppress the wrong ones. Our censors are the unsung heroes of intelligence."

## How Censors Learn

Censors develop through experience, especially through failure. When a line of reasoning leads to a dead end, the mind can form a censor that recognizes the beginning of that line and blocks it in the future. When a social behavior leads to embarrassment, a censor forms that recognizes similar situations and suppresses the behavior. This is the negative counterpart of positive learning (Chapter 10): instead of learning what to do, the mind learns what not to do.

The development of censors explains why experienced thinkers are more efficient than novices. It is not just that experts know more — they also have better censors that prune unproductive lines of thought before they waste time and effort. A master chess player does not consider most possible moves — her censors have learned to suppress obviously bad moves, leaving only the promising ones for conscious evaluation.

## The Cognitive Basis of Humor

Minsky offers a fascinating theory of humor based on censors. A joke works by leading the listener toward a particular interpretation — building up expectations through a setup — and then suddenly introducing an element that violates those expectations. The punchline activates a censor that recognizes the setup was heading in a wrong or forbidden direction, but the surprise of the reversal produces pleasure rather than suppression.

Why is this pleasurable? Minsky suggests that the pleasure of humor is related to the pleasure of learning. When a censor fires unexpectedly — when we suddenly see that our thinking was on a wrong track — we experience a small cognitive insight. We learn something about the limitations of our own thinking. Humor, in this view, is a reward for recognizing errors in our own mental processes.

This explains several features of humor. Jokes are funnier when they are surprising — a predictable punchline is not funny because the censor fires too early. Jokes are funnier when they involve taboo subjects — social censors are among the strongest, and circumventing them produces the greatest surprise. And jokes are less funny on repetition — once the censor has learned the joke, there is no surprise.

## Self-Regulation and Mental Health

Censors play a crucial role in mental health and self-regulation. Effective censors allow the mind to function smoothly, suppressing distracting thoughts, preventing impulsive actions, and maintaining focus on current goals. But censors can malfunction in various ways.

An underactive censor system can lead to impulsivity, poor judgment, and difficulty filtering out irrelevant thoughts. An overactive censor system can lead to rigidity, anxiety, and the suppression of creative or novel ideas. Obsessive thoughts may result from a failure of specific censors — the mind cannot suppress a recurring idea that it recognizes as unproductive but cannot block.

Understanding the censor system is essential for understanding both the successes and the failures of the society of mind. Censors are what give the mind its discipline, its judgment, and — through the surprising mechanism of humor — its capacity for delight.
