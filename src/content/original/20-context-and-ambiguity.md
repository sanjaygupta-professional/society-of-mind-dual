# Context and Ambiguity

## The Ubiquity of Ambiguity

Almost everything we encounter is ambiguous. The word "bank" can mean a financial institution or the edge of a river. A smile can express happiness, politeness, or contempt. A raised hand can be a greeting, a question, or a threat. The world is saturated with ambiguity, yet we usually navigate it without difficulty. How?

The answer is context. The same signal — a word, a gesture, a visual pattern — activates different agents depending on what other agents are already active. If you are reading about fishing, "bank" activates river-related agents. If you are reading about finance, it activates money-related agents. Context is not a single thing but the entire pattern of currently active agents, which biases the interpretation of incoming signals toward contextually appropriate meanings.

> "Nothing means anything by itself. Everything depends on the context — which is another way of saying that meaning arises not from individual agents but from the relationships among all the agents that are active at a given moment."

## How Context Works

Context operates through a mechanism Minsky calls **priming**. When certain agents are active, they increase the readiness of related agents to become active, while decreasing the readiness of unrelated agents. If you have been thinking about animals, the word "bat" is more likely to activate agents for a flying mammal than agents for a baseball bat. The priming agents work in the background, outside awareness, shaping interpretation before the conscious mind has a chance to consider alternatives.

This explains why context effects are so rapid and automatic. You do not consciously deliberate about which meaning of "bank" to choose — the priming agents have already made the choice for you by the time the word reaches awareness. Only when context is insufficient — when the priming is ambiguous — do you experience conscious uncertainty about meaning.

## Frames and Default Expectations

Context is closely related to Minsky's theory of frames (Chapter 24). A frame is a knowledge structure that represents a typical situation — a restaurant, a birthday party, a classroom. When a frame is activated, it provides a rich context that resolves many potential ambiguities at once. In a restaurant frame, "check" means a bill, "menu" means a list of food options, and "table" means a dining surface rather than a data structure.

Frames provide default expectations — assumptions about what is likely to be true in a given situation. These defaults can be overridden by specific information, but in the absence of such information, they fill in the gaps and resolve ambiguities. This is enormously efficient, because it allows the mind to understand situations quickly without having to resolve every ambiguity from scratch.

## Multiple Interpretations

Sometimes ambiguity is not resolved — the context supports multiple interpretations simultaneously. Minsky argues that in such cases, the mind may maintain multiple interpretations in parallel, at least for a time. Different agents may be pursuing different interpretations, and the competition among them is resolved only when additional information tips the balance.

This parallel processing of multiple interpretations is evident in humor (Chapter 27) and in creative thinking (Chapter 14). Jokes often work by leading the listener toward one interpretation and then suddenly switching to another. Creative insights often come from recognizing that a situation supports an unexpected interpretation. In both cases, the mind's ability to maintain and switch between multiple interpretations is essential.

## The Power and Danger of Context

Context is a powerful tool, but it can also mislead. Stereotypes, prejudices, and cognitive biases are all cases where contextual expectations override the evidence of the actual situation. If you expect a certain kind of person to behave in a certain way, your context-setting agents may cause you to interpret ambiguous behavior as confirming your expectation, even when it does not.

Understanding how context works — and how it can go wrong — is essential for understanding the mind. Context is not just a background condition for thought; it is one of the most active and important processes in the society of mind.
