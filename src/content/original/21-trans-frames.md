# Trans-Frames

## Understanding Change

The world is not static. Things move, transform, appear, and disappear. People act, objects change state, situations evolve. How does the mind represent and reason about change? Minsky introduces **trans-frames** — knowledge structures specifically designed to represent transitions from one state to another. A trans-frame captures a before-state, an after-state, and the relationship between them — what changed, what caused the change, and what remained the same.

When you see someone pick up a cup, your mind constructs a trans-frame: before, the cup was on the table and the hand was empty; after, the cup is in the hand and the table is bare. The trans-frame also captures the cause (the person's intention and action) and the invariants (the cup itself did not change, only its location).

> "To understand any event, you need to know what changed, what stayed the same, and what made the difference. Trans-frames are the mind's way of organizing this knowledge."

## Before and After

The simplest trans-frames represent before/after pairs. A door goes from closed to open. A light goes from off to on. A person goes from sitting to standing. These simple transitions are the building blocks of more complex understanding. Sequences of events are chains of trans-frames, each connecting to the next: the person stands up (trans-frame 1), walks to the door (trans-frame 2), opens it (trans-frame 3), and leaves (trans-frame 4).

Minsky notes that our understanding of time itself may be built from trans-frames. We do not experience time as a continuous flow but as a sequence of states connected by transitions. The feeling of "before" and "after" is generated by trans-frame agents that keep track of which states preceded which.

This connects to Chapter 24's frame theory. Frames represent static situations; trans-frames represent transitions between situations. Together, they provide the machinery for understanding both the stable and the changing aspects of the world.

## Spatial Transformations

Many trans-frames involve spatial transformations — movements, rotations, and changes in perspective. When you walk around a table, the table looks different from each angle, but you understand that it is the same table seen from different viewpoints. This understanding requires trans-frames that connect different spatial perspectives to the same underlying object.

Minsky connects this to Chapter 25's discussion of frame arrays. A frame array is a set of frames connected by spatial transformations — each frame represents the object from a different angle, and the trans-frames connecting them represent the movements needed to go from one angle to another. This system allows the mind to predict what something will look like from an angle not yet experienced, which is essential for navigation, manipulation, and spatial reasoning.

## Cause and Effect

Trans-frames also capture causal relationships. When you push a ball and it rolls, the trans-frame includes not just the before-state (ball stationary) and after-state (ball rolling) but also the causal link (pushing caused rolling). Causal understanding requires agents that can identify which changes are connected — which actions produce which effects — and this is one of the most important and difficult tasks in cognition.

Minsky notes that children develop causal understanding gradually (Chapter 17). An infant may notice that crying brings a caregiver, but the infant's understanding of the causal mechanism is limited. Over time, the child develops more sophisticated trans-frames that capture increasingly complex causal relationships, eventually supporting the kind of hypothetical reasoning described in Chapter 18: "if I do X, then Y will happen."

Trans-frames are one of the fundamental representational tools of the society of mind — the structures that allow us to understand not just what the world is like, but how and why it changes.
