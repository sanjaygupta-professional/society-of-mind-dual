# Development

## How Minds Grow

Minds do not arrive fully formed. They grow, change, and reorganize throughout childhood and beyond. Minsky draws heavily on the work of Jean Piaget, who showed that children's thinking passes through distinct stages, each with its own characteristic abilities and limitations. But Minsky reinterprets Piaget's stages through the lens of the society of mind: each stage represents a major reorganization of the agent society, in which new kinds of agents and new kinds of connections become available.

An infant's mind is dominated by sensorimotor agents — those that coordinate perception and action in the immediate present. The infant can reach for a toy, track a moving object, and learn simple cause-and-effect relationships. But the infant cannot represent absent objects, plan for the future, or think abstractly. These abilities require agents that have not yet developed.

> "The mind grows not by simply accumulating more knowledge, but by developing entirely new kinds of mental machinery — new agents, new connections, new ways of organizing thought."

## Piaget's Stages Reinterpreted

Piaget described four major stages of cognitive development: sensorimotor, preoperational, concrete operational, and formal operational. Minsky reinterprets each as a phase in the development of the agent society.

In the **sensorimotor stage** (birth to age two), the child's agents are tied to direct physical interaction. Knowledge is encoded in action patterns rather than mental representations. In the **preoperational stage** (ages two to seven), new representational agents emerge, allowing the child to use symbols and language, but reasoning is still limited by the inability to coordinate multiple perspectives simultaneously.

The **concrete operational stage** (ages seven to eleven) brings agents that can perform mental operations on concrete objects — reversing actions, classifying, ordering. Finally, the **formal operational stage** (age twelve and beyond) introduces agents capable of abstract, hypothetical reasoning — thinking about thinking, reasoning about possibilities that have never been experienced.

## The Mechanism of Stage Transitions

What drives the transition from one stage to the next? Minsky suggests it is not simply biological maturation, though that plays a role. Stage transitions occur when the mind's existing agents are unable to handle the problems it faces, creating pressure for reorganization. The child who cannot conserve quantity is troubled by the discrepancy between appearance and reality; this trouble activates learning processes that eventually produce new agents capable of representing conservation.

This connects to Papert's Principle (Chapter 10): the child cannot learn conservation until the right representational structures are available. The transition happens when the accumulation of experience, combined with biological readiness, creates the conditions for new agents to form and new connections to be established.

## Development Never Stops

Although Piaget focused on childhood, Minsky emphasizes that cognitive development continues throughout life. Adults develop new ways of thinking as they encounter new domains and challenges. A physicist develops agents for thinking about quantum mechanics. A musician develops agents for hearing complex harmonies. An experienced manager develops agents for reading social dynamics.

These later developments may not represent wholesale stage transitions, but they share the same underlying process: new agents form, new connections are established, and the society of mind is reorganized to accommodate new capabilities. The mind is never finished. It is always under construction, always developing new ways to understand and interact with the world.

This chapter shows that the society of mind is not a static structure but a dynamic, evolving system — one that grows through a series of reorganizations, each building on and transforming what came before.
