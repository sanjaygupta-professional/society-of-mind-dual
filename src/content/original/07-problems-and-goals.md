# Problems and Goals

## The Nature of Goals

What does it mean to have a goal? In the society of mind, a goal is not a single thought or desire held by a unified self. It is a state maintained by certain agents that activates other agents to bring about a particular condition. When you are hungry, the goal of eating is maintained by agents that monitor blood sugar, stomach signals, and time of day. These agents activate food-seeking agents, which in turn activate navigation agents, hand-movement agents, and so on. The goal persists as long as the maintaining agents remain active.

This view explains several puzzling features of goals. Goals can conflict (Chapter 3), because different maintaining agents can be active simultaneously. Goals can be forgotten, because the maintaining agents can be suppressed by other agents. And goals can change suddenly, because a new signal — a loud noise, an unexpected sight — can activate new maintaining agents that override the old ones.

> "A goal is not a thing in the mind — it is a condition maintained by agents, which other agents work to satisfy."

## Subgoals and Problem Decomposition

Most interesting problems cannot be solved in a single step. They must be broken down into subproblems, each with its own subgoal. This is what Minsky calls **problem decomposition**, and it is one of the mind's most powerful strategies.

Consider the goal of building a house. This decomposes into subgoals: lay a foundation, frame the walls, install a roof, and so on. Each of these further decomposes: laying a foundation requires digging, pouring concrete, and letting it cure. The decomposition continues until the subgoals are simple enough for individual agents to handle.

But decomposition is not always straightforward. Sometimes the subgoals interact — you cannot install the roof until the walls are framed, and you cannot frame the walls until the foundation is laid. Managing these interdependencies requires yet another level of agents — planning agents that keep track of which subgoals have been achieved and which are still pending.

## Planning and the Problem of Search

How does the mind find solutions to problems? One approach is search — systematically trying different actions and seeing which ones lead toward the goal. But search is expensive. The number of possible actions grows exponentially with each step, and brute-force search quickly becomes impractical.

The mind uses many tricks to make search manageable. One is to use heuristics — rules of thumb that suggest promising directions without guaranteeing success. Another is to use analogies — recognizing that the current problem resembles one solved before and adapting the old solution. A third is to work backward from the goal, asking "What would need to be true just before the goal is achieved?" and then treating that condition as a new subgoal.

## When Goals Fail

Not all goals can be achieved, and recognizing failure is itself an important skill. The mind needs agents that detect when a goal is unreachable — when the search has gone on too long, when the subgoals are contradictory, or when the resources are insufficient. These failure-detecting agents trigger a switch to alternative strategies: trying a different approach, reformulating the problem (Chapter 14), or abandoning the goal entirely.

Minsky emphasizes that failure is not just an obstacle to intelligence — it is essential to learning. When a strategy fails, the mind has an opportunity to learn which approaches do not work, and this negative knowledge (explored in Chapter 10 through Papert's Principle) is often more valuable than knowing what does work. The society of mind grows not just through success, but through the intelligent management of failure.
