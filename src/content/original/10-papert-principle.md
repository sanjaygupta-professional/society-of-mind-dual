# Papert's Principle

## The Right Way to Learn

Seymour Papert, Minsky's longtime colleague at MIT, articulated a principle that Minsky considers essential to understanding learning: **you cannot learn something unless you already have the right representations to express it**. This is Papert's Principle, and it has profound implications for how we think about education, child development, and artificial intelligence.

Consider trying to learn calculus without knowing algebra. It is not just difficult — it is impossible, because the concepts of calculus are expressed in terms of algebraic concepts. Without the algebraic representations, the calculus concepts have nowhere to "land" in the mind. Similarly, a child cannot learn the concept of conservation — that the amount of water stays the same when poured into a differently shaped glass — until the child has developed the right representational structures to express the relationship between quantity and shape.

> "Some of the most crucial steps in mental growth are not learning new skills but learning new ways to represent things — new ways that make it possible to learn things that were previously unlearnable."

## Debugging the Mind

Minsky draws a striking analogy between learning and debugging computer programs. When a program fails, the programmer does not start over from scratch — she examines the failure, identifies the bug, and fixes it. Learning from failure works the same way. When a strategy does not work, the mind needs agents that can diagnose why it failed and suggest specific repairs.

This is harder than it sounds, because failures can have many causes. Did the strategy fail because the goal was wrong, because the method was wrong, because a crucial fact was missing, or because the representation was inadequate? Different kinds of failure require different kinds of repair, and the mind needs specialized agents for each kind of diagnosis.

Minsky emphasizes that learning from failure is more informative than learning from success. Success tells you that something worked, but not why. Failure tells you specifically what does not work, which constrains the space of possibilities and guides the search for better approaches.

## Learning New Representations

The most important kind of learning is not acquiring new facts or new skills but acquiring new representations — new ways of thinking about things. When a child learns to count, she does not just learn the names of numbers; she acquires a new representational system that makes it possible to think about quantity, order, and arithmetic. This representational change opens up vast new territories of thought that were previously inaccessible.

Minsky connects this to the developmental stages described by Piaget (Chapter 17). Each stage represents the acquisition of new representational capabilities — from sensorimotor representations in infancy to formal operational representations in adolescence. Each new kind of representation makes possible new kinds of learning, which in turn can lead to still newer representations.

## The Teacher's Dilemma

Papert's Principle creates a dilemma for teachers: you cannot simply present information and expect students to learn it. You must first ensure that students have the representational structures needed to make sense of the information. This often means teaching representations before teaching content — a subtlety that is lost in many educational approaches.

The principle also explains why some things are so hard to learn. Not because they are inherently complex, but because the leap from the learner's current representations to the required ones is too large. Effective learning requires building bridges — intermediate representations that connect what the learner already knows to what the learner needs to know. This is the art of teaching and the foundation of cognitive growth.
